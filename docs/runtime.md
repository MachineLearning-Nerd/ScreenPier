The correction queues changed control availability on the next ordinary capture turn and preserves the periodic deadline. The same private reproduction now delivers the literal `z`. ## Streaming and input mechanisms

Cooperative capture owns one Wayland operation with an original 120 ms deadline. It yields while waiting for Ready so native input and cleanup can run. Periodic lock queries wait until capture is no longer pending. Callback timing, geometry generation, and frame ownership remain checked. This removes a demonstrated input-blocking wait. It does not impose a hard bound on every native operation.

The encoder selects the latest unstarted raw frame. Once any frame byte is written, that frame and its identity remain immutable until completion or failure. Encoded frames retain their order. A packet-length and checksum ledger avoids waiting for the next H.264 access-unit delimiter. Hardware async depth is one. Real encoder tests establish correct framing and decode; they do not establish browser speed or readability.

Native input authority uses a revision and explicit release acknowledgement. Queued ordinary key releases preserve shortcut order. Urgent cleanup revokes admission and releases held input. A browser must release held keys before continuing after a reset. Clipboard availability uses current display and lock state separately from keyboard focus.

The host retains the 500 ms changed-content limit, 750 ms capture-health limit, and three-second display-acknowledgement and heartbeat limits. A stale displayed image pauses input. These limits have not been widened to hide delays. Browser media recovery has separate visible-tab and matched-presentation timing, retains the existing one-minute recovery bound, and never replays input.

Permanent diagnostics use a bounded background writer and an owner-only file. They record numeric timing and counters without keys, clipboard text, pixels, credentials, addresses, or negotiation data. Latest timing samples are not distributions. Desktop actor counters accumulate across sessions, so a session's first default sample is not a cumulative baseline. Backend completion does not prove target-application receipt.

## Current quality-mode evidence

Three guarded zero-loss 1 Mbps route-change runs of the installed binary held Clear text at 800 kbps / 15 fps. All nine native 14 px crops matched literal OCR, there were no proxy drops, and none of the 54 constrained-phase samples showed Waiting. Three separate zero-loss 0.5 Mbps Smooth-to-Low-to-Smooth runs with queue-preserving handover had readable 14 px crops. The two repeated runs showed 0–1 of 18 constrained samples in Waiting; restored Smooth fell behind and automatically returned to Clear text, leaving 3 of 20 restored samples in Waiting. A single earlier handover run behaved similarly. Later tests with the proposed slow-link delay and loss still produced Waiting, lost literal input, and an unsuccessful Smooth-to-Low handover. The fixed profiles improve the zero-loss local case but do not yet pass slow-link reliability, automatic rate adaptation, the Smooth target, or Mac quality acceptance. ## Performance acceptance remains open

The following paragraphs record earlier experiments against older binaries. Statements that an experimental selector was isolated or the pipe build was running describe the state when those experiments occurred; the current installed binary is identified above.

On 14 October, five alternating isolated physical-fixture pairs compared the previous input pipe against a best-effort 1 MiB pipe. Median raw-frame write time fell from 89 to 11 ms, and median presented-frame age from 532.5 to 357 ms. Restored-phase waiting fell from 10 to 1 of 20 samples. All ten restored 14 px text crops matched literal OCR. The 700 kbps constrained phase still had frequent waiting, and some pipe runs dropped more queued packets. Other compile load varied during these runs. [The paired result](../.audit/reliability-quality/pipe-five-pairs.md) supported a private Mac smoke test, not low-bandwidth acceptance. The pipe build passed 170 Rust tests, three real encoder checks, 67 browser assertions, formatting, and Clippy. That build ran at the same private URL before the quality-mode switch. The previous `d283` executable is preserved at `.audit/runtime/backup-d283bbf094f2a6d7`.

Two further guarded local screens used that exact private binary at 1 Mbps and 0.5 Mbps. They finished without proxy packet drops but failed literal 14 px text OCR during and after the constrained phase. The restored host bitrate remained near 200 kbps. The 1 Mbps phase showed Waiting in 7 of 18 samples; the 0.5 Mbps phase was visually blurred despite zero constrained waiting samples. [The exact result](../.audit/reliability-quality/pipe-current-screen.md) leaves both weak-link targets open.

Further isolated fixed-rate screens exposed a flaw in the OCR measurement: the previous nearest-neighbor enlargement made some clean native `0` glyphs read as `8`. Native-pixel OCR and direct crop inspection pass all three phases of two fixed 800 kbps / 15 fps 1 Mbps runs and one 0.5 Mbps run on the controlled fixture. Native OCR also passes all three repeated constrained 560 kbps / 8 fps 1 Mbps crops; earlier OCR-only rejections of that recipe are superseded. Fixed 400 kbps / 8 fps is still unreliable at 1 Mbps. A fixed 800 kbps floor failed at 100 kbps, with 968 total proxy queue drops and video presentation stopping. The isolated 5 Mbps animation screen reached only about 13–19 presented fps while host load exceeded 11 and capture took a median of roughly 47 ms. These are local screens, not a deployable adaptive policy or Mac acceptance. [Trial details and original receipts](../.audit/reliability-quality/native-ocr-and-rate-screen.md) record the limits.

An isolated peer-statistics build then measured zero outbound loss throughout a 0.5 Mbps fixed-800 profile even while the estimator dipped below 400 kbps. At 100 kbps, reported outbound loss reached 45–91 percent, RTT climbed, and video stopped. [The sender-side screen](../.audit/reliability-quality/linkstats-screen.md) motivates an experimental loss-aware selector; that selector is not running on the private service.

The first opt-in loss-aware selector failed its 0.5 Mbps and 100 kbps route-change screens. It promoted to 2400 kbps during the initial 5 Mbps phase, then flooded the narrower route with queued video and probes and reduced to an unreadable 100 kbps recipe. [The failed prototype](../.audit/reliability-quality/quality-prototype-v1.md) remains isolated. A safe selector needs recipe lifecycle and sender-queue evidence, not only a loss threshold.

Two further isolated selectors added timestamped sender-queue observations and matched displayed-frame acknowledgements to encoder instances. Both still failed the 0.5 Mbps route-change gate: v2 made a second reduction on an uncorroborated RTT spike; v3 removed that trigger but still showed Waiting in 15 of 18 samples with repeated encoder replacements and 343 proxy drops. [The v2 receipt](../.audit/reliability-quality/quality-prototype-v2.md) and [v3 receipt](../.audit/reliability-quality/quality-prototype-v3.md) rule out deploying either. They do not change the reviewed private binary.

Five further isolated route-change designs also failed the 0.5 Mbps availability gate. Clearing old RTP, holding a measured 560 kbps / 8 fps recipe, pausing media for two seconds, resetting the WebRTC estimator, and starting its new estimate below the media budget reduced some sender-side symptoms but left 7–17 of 18 constrained samples in Waiting in the first trials. A repeat of the pause candidate improved to 6 of 18; that variation is not acceptance. A fixed 560 kbps screen with no high-to-low transition had zero Waiting samples and exact native 14 px OCR. The difference implicates the route-change burst and subsequent browser/decoder recovery rather than steady-state narrow-link capacity. [The v4–v8 report](../.audit/reliability-quality/quality-prototypes-v4-v8.md) contains exact hashes, receipts, and limits. None was deployed.

The original Tailscale Serve route at port 443 still points to local port 3000. On 14 October it returned HTTP 502 because no process was listening on port 3000. That backend is outside ScreenPier; its state before the private service switch was not recorded. The 8443 route and ScreenPier itself returned HTTP 200.
